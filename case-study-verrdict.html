VERRDICT

Designing trust into network governance.

A large financial company tracked its network risk across three tools, and joined the results by hand in spreadsheets and email. I designed VERRDICT to put those checks in one place, so a reviewer can explain every decision they make.

00Overview

When you can’t see how a decision was made, every decision is a risk.

Challenge

Vendor advisories, the asset inventory and change records lived in separate tools. Reviewers couldn’t tell what to look at first, or why a device had been flagged.

What I did

Audited the existing tools, interviewed the team and compared other tools. That produced three insights about attention, trust and continuity, and five design decisions built on them.

Result

Six modules on one shared record, where every verdict shows its reasons. Delivered to the brief and moving toward adoption. There are no live usage numbers yet.

01The problem

Every decision was pieced together by hand

Network governance is a loop, not a report. Devices are tracked, vendor warnings arrive, someone reaches a verdict, and that verdict has to hold up in an audit. If one link breaks, nobody can explain the result.

In the old setup the loop ran through spreadsheets. Two people could look at the same device and reach different conclusions, with nothing to show which was right.

Problem statement

The work was spread across tools, and the reasoning was hidden.

A data problem → a clarity problem. Reviewers didn’t need more data. They needed to see how each verdict was reached.

Every tool shows the risk. Almost none show the reasoning behind it. The missing piece wasn’t more data. It was the link between the data and the judgement.

What I asked the team: Where do you go first? What makes you distrust a number? What do you show someone who challenges you? What would you stop doing in a spreadsheet?

What I learned · three insights
A · Attention
Reviewers didn’t need more alerts. They needed to know which one to open first.

Severity doesn’t tell you what to do first, and 452 alerts that all look alike cause alert fatigue.

Led toA ranked attention queue → Decisions 01, 04
B · Trust
A verdict without a reason is a liability, not an answer.

Flags that came without a reason were escalated instead of resolved. This finding gave the product its name.

Led toEvery verdict shows its reasons → Decision 02
C · Continuity
Where data came from mattered as much as what it said.

Vendor notices and internal records looked identical. Reviewers read the data in one tool and fixed problems in another.

Led toSource labels, fixes raised from the record → Decisions 03, 05
03Approach

Reframing the brief

How might we make every decision something a reviewer can act on, and explain to someone who wasn’t there, without leaving one place?

Four principles I designed against
  1. P1

    Explainable by default

    No status ships unless its reason is one click away.

  2. P2

    Priority over volume

    Rank the work. A count on its own tells a reviewer nothing.

  3. P3

    Label the source

    Say whether it’s external or internal, live or a snapshot, and how old it is.

  4. P4

    Calm at density

    Colour is reserved for risk. Nothing else competes for attention.

04Key UX decisions

Five decisions, and what each one cost

Each decision answers a finding from research. For each, I’ve noted the obvious option I turned down, and the trade-off I accepted.

DECISION 01P2 · Priority over volume

Tell the reviewer what to open first

Problem
452 alerts, all equally loud. Reviewers didn’t know where to start.
Chose
A short list of four, ranked by business impact and deadline, with an action on every row.
Not
Better filters, or sorting the table by severity. Filters only help someone who already knows what to look for.
Cost
Reviewers have to trust a ranking they didn’t set, so the ranking rule is printed under the list.
  1. 1“What needs attention”, ranked 1–4
  2. 2Every row carries its verb
  3. 3Data age in the header
DECISION 02P1 · Explainable by default

Show the working, not just the verdict

Problem
“Gap” with no reason attached. Unexplained flags were escalated, not resolved.
Chose
Every verdict opens into the checks behind it: Implementation, Test, Tier-2, then the verdict.
Not
A methodology document, or a tooltip on the badge.
Cost
Denser rows, and every status needs its evidence stored.
  1. 1Three checks, then the verdict
  2. 2Evidence gaps counted separately
  3. 3Source of record and last sync, named
DECISION 03P3 · Label the source

Say where every number came from

Problem
Vendor advisories and internal records looked the same, so reviewers treated them as equally reliable.
Chose
Each feed says whether it’s external or internal, live or a snapshot, and when it last ran.
Not
One “updated 2 min ago” stamp in the header.
Cost
More small labels on screen, which is why colour stays reserved for risk.
  1. 1External and internal are separate tabs
  2. 2Live or snapshot, per feed
  3. 31,744 loaded → 6 relevant, shown as a visible narrowing
DECISION 04P4 · Calm at density

Warn eighteen months before the deadline

Problem
Alerts fired on the end-of-life date, but replacing a device takes approvals, a build, testing and a release window.
Chose
Alerts start 18 months out and step up as the date nears. Each severity names the next action, from “roadmap it” to “remediate or except it.”
Not
Alerting on the EOL date, or only showing the date.
Cost
452 open alerts instead of a handful. That only works because the queue is ranked (Decision 01).
  1. 1One banner, for the single worst device
  2. 2Breached, shown apart from the 18-month look-ahead
  3. 3Ranking rules and audit ledger one tab away
DECISION 05Close the loop

Let the reviewer act without leaving the record

Problem
The decision and the fix lived in different tools. Evidence was retyped, and the reasoning stayed behind.
Chose
Raise the Jira ticket from the verdict itself, pre-filled with the evidence, and track it back to closed.
Not
Exporting a list and raising the ticket by hand.
Cost
Jira’s status can disagree with ours, so every record names the system it last heard from, and when.
  1. 1“Raise ticket” sits on the verdict, not in a toolbar
  2. 2Assignee, status and age read back from Jira
  3. 3When the two disagree, say when we last heard
05System & quality

Colour is only for risk

With this many alerts on screen, colour had to mean one thing. Everything structural stays in navy and blue. Red, amber and green appear only when there’s a risk, and always with a word next to them.

Tokens
  • Critical / Gap
  • High / Needs review
  • Compliant

Handed over as named tokens. No screen picks its own red.

States nobody demos
  • Empty: “0 fully compliant” still shows the week-on-week comparison.
  • Loading: each source refreshes on its own, so the screen never goes blank.
  • Stale: every feed shows when it last ran. Stale data is labelled, not hidden.
  • Critical: one banner, never two. Only the worst breach gets it.
Accessibility
  • 1.4.1Status carries a word: PASS, FAIL, REVIEW, BREACHED, Gap.
  • 1.4.3Contrast: body and heading text clear 4.5:1.
  • 2.1Keyboard: row actions are real buttons with a focus ring.
  • DensitySerial numbers in mono, so they read character by character.

06Outcome & reflection

Outcome and reflection

Built to the brief, well received, and moving toward adoption.

Delivered

Six modules, a component set, and flows checked against the agreed criteria.

Status

This was a design project. There are no live usage numbers yet, and I haven’t made any up.

Next

Test the ranking rules on live data, then measure time to first decision.

Against the brief
  • Consolidate governance into one view→Six modules under one shell
  • Track advisories and lifecycle milestones→Per-source feeds matched to assets
  • Verify changes against evidence→Three-stage assurance, gaps tracked
  • Report exposure to compliance→Trends with a named primary exposure
What I learned
In regulated work, the interface is the argument.

If a screen can’t explain itself, the explanation ends up in email and people’s memory. Building the reasoning into the screens was the product.

What I’d do differently
Test the ranking with real reviewers sooner.

The whole queue depends on reviewers trusting the ranking. Next, I’d validate the rules on live data and design the escalation path.
