7-Eleven operates 13,000+ stores across the US, where store managers, area managers, and corporate teams juggle inventory, IoT device health, staffing, and compliance — across 4 disconnected legacy systems, every single day.

The goal was a centralized AI-powered operations platform — one unified surface serving 4 distinct roles with real-time visibility, intelligent prioritization, and actionable workflows.

◆ THE PROBLEM

What was broken?

Store managers were drowning in noise. The tools meant to help them run operations were fragmented, reactive, and impossible to act on fast.

🔔
Pain 01
Every alert looked the same

No severity hierarchy. A spill notice and a freezer failure had identical visual weight — managers couldn't triage at a glance and critical issues got buried.

🔧
Pain 02
4 tools, 4 logins, zero context

Inventory, staffing, compliance, and incident reporting lived in completely separate systems. Switching between them mid-shift meant losing time and mental context.

⏱
Pain 03
Reactive, never proactive

Managers discovered problems from customers or after damage was done — not from the system. There was no mechanism to surface emerging issues before they escalated.

📋
Pain 04
No accountability trail

When something went wrong, there was no record of who saw the alert or what action was taken. Compliance audits were painful manual reconstructions.

◆ GOALS

what we set out to do

Three goals shaped the redesign. Not all were equally visible to managers — all mattered to the business.

"Why do I find out about problems last?"

"How do we cut resolution time across 13,000+ stores?"

"Can one dashboard replace four different logins?"

🎯
Reduce alert fatigue through smart prioritization

Give critical alerts visual dominance so managers immediately know what needs attention, and what can wait.

🎯
Cut average time-to-resolution by surfacing context immediately

Managers shouldn't need to switch apps to understand an alert. Everything needed to resolve an issue should appear in-context.

🎯

Four logins, four mental models, four places to miss something — collapse this into one coherent daily view.

◆ APPROACH

key product decisions

Before designing screens, I worked through the decisions that would shape the whole experience. A few mattered more than the rest, and each one came with a tradeoff.

📍
Severity should drive visual hierarchy, not chronology

Newest-first buried a freezer failure under a fresh parking lot report. We inverted the sort: severity is primary, recency secondary. Resolution speed improved immediately.

📍
The dashboard should reflect what managers do, not what the system tracks

The system tracked 40+ data points. Managers acted on 6. We ruthlessly filtered to the manager's mental model — less data, dramatically faster decisions.

📍
Alerts should resolve in-context, not require app-switching

We collapsed a 5-step cross-app workflow into: see alert → tap → resolve → auto-logged. More complexity in one view, zero app-switching in the real workflow.

📍
Accountability should be automatic, not manually logged

Managers skipped manual logging under pressure. Auto-logging every resolution action pushed audit trail completion from 40% to 100% — with zero added friction.

◆ PRIORITIZATION

the most urgent thing, first

Alert fatigue isn't a volume problem — it's a hierarchy problem. When everything looks the same, nothing gets treated as urgent. We redesigned the alert list so severity is always the primary sort, with color, numbering, and visual weight reinforcing the order.

The decision. We chose to hide low-priority alerts behind a "Show all" toggle by default. Managers resisted at first — they worried about missing things. After two weeks, every manager in testing preferred it. Nothing important was missed; routine items were handled faster because critical items got handled first.
◆ UNIFIED CONTEXT

every system, one screen

The freezer alert and the inventory shortage are the same problem — a failed cooler means dairy spoilage which means a stock gap. The old system showed these as three separate notifications in three separate apps. We surfaced the connected context in a single view.

The decision. We pulled live inventory data into alert cards so managers could see the downstream impact of an issue immediately. The technical lift was significant — it required a real-time data bridge between four legacy systems — but resolution time dropped by 32% because managers stopped switching apps to gather context.

◆ REFLECTIONS

reflections & learnings

  • The most impactful decisions came from rethinking workflows, not redesigning screens.
  • Alert fatigue is a hierarchy problem, not a volume problem.
  • Consolidating systems mattered more to trust than visual polish.
  • The best operational tools disappear into the background until they're needed.
A note on this project

The hardest part wasn't designing the interface — it was convincing stakeholders that removing features (four apps down to one) was the right move. Every team wanted their data front and center. The real design challenge was negotiating information hierarchy across organizations, not pixels on a screen.
